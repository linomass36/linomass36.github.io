How it is put together

A place can hold several panels

Tabs across the top of a place move between its panels. Some places keep all their panels in one file; others keep them as separate pages and show the same tab bar on each. You cannot tell from using it, which is the point — the difference exists because merging some of them would have risked breaking them, not because it means anything to you.

A floor day, and the one thing it asks for

Three days with nothing logged, or a condition you declared as stopping you, and the Standing becomes a floor day: the board folds away, nothing is counted as owed, no streak breaks, and the page asks for one thing. The backlog is still there and still reachable — show me the numbers anyway — but you have to ask for it.

That one thing is read out of your own stores and named. It is the item off today's list, or the block whose loop is open, or twenty of the cards actually waiting, with a link straight to where it is done and two alternatives under it. It used to be a single line of fixed prose — "pick the smallest open loop and shut it" — which named nothing, counted nothing, linked nowhere, and was impossible on a hub with no open loops.

An open loop, since the word is used: a block in the closure log you have studied but never scored. Reading it once and putting the two scores in is what closes it.

Today's list is yours, and it does not follow you

Every other list here belongs to a system — the plan owns its moves, the board owns its sessions. Today's list, at the top of Today, is the one nothing owns: ring the bank, collect the parcel, the things that used to live in a notes app. Type into it there, or press ⌘K on any page and start the line with ! — it lands on today from wherever you were standing.

What you do not finish is not carried forward automatically, ever. The next day you open the hub, the most recent unfinished day is offered back — at most three items, with the number it is not offering said out loud. Take them or clear them; either way that day stops asking. This is the same posture as the floor day and the anatomy re-entry ramp: the hub's reply to a gap is a smaller ask, never a longer list. Miss a fortnight and there is nothing to come back to but three lines and the option to bin them.

The days themselves are kept as written. An item you carried is marked carried and an item you dropped is marked dropped — neither is erased, so a week you can read back still says what you meant to do on the Tuesday.

Red means owed

Arterial red means something wants you today, and it means nothing else here. Links are the blue-grey. The previous version used one colour for both, which is why a board of thirteen tiles read as an emergency in which nothing could be prioritised.

Names, two ways

The pages have written names — the Standing, the Examiner, Recalibrate. The drawer has a Plain names switch that swaps them for plain ones and keeps the written name underneath, so you can learn one from the other. It syncs between your devices.

The Season is a gate, not a diary

Under People, beside the Wait, because it is the same kind of object one size up: a bounded posture with a start date, an edge and a fixed review. Nothing on it is remembered that can be worked out. The day number, the check-in days, the end date and what tonight costs all come from the single moment you started it — so a season that ran out cannot go on claiming to be live, and the page says abandoned rather than quietly carrying on.

It can start tomorrow, and usually should. The declaration is written tonight; the season is lived from the morning. Starting the clock at 22:00 spends day one on a day that is already over — so tomorrow morning is the default, and a season declared but not yet begun reads as pending: the rules and tonight's bedtime, nothing owed.

Pressed the wrong one at the wrong hour? If the start time ate most of the day, the page leads with the question rather than with advice for a day that is over, and offers both answers: move it, or keep today and stop being asked. It stays a question — the hub does not rewrite a stored date behind you, because one the app changed quietly is worse than one you corrected yourself.

While day one has not run, moving the start is a correction — the date moves, the change is stamped, and nothing is archived. Filing a season that lasted an hour would turn the archive into a list of typos. Past day one it is a restart again, and it costs a row.

And on the day a season begins, any block whose hour had already passed is marked not asked rather than missed. Grey means you did not say; this means it was never yours to do. A card that demands a 06:05 manuscript block from a season that started at 22:14 is asking for something its own start time made impossible.

Tonight is arithmetic, and the calendar sets it. Phone away, lights out, wake and leave are computed backwards from your first fixed commitment, through the morning chain and the commute — read from the Week, both the blocks pulled from Google and the ones you typed in yourself. Not from a stored shift time: shifts vary, and a 07:00 hike before a 13:30 shift decides the alarm completely. The card names the block it anchored on, so the number is an argument you can check rather than a demand.

It anchors on the NEXT time you have to be up, which after today's first commitment has passed means tomorrow's — tonight's bedtime is decided by tomorrow morning, not by a day that is already running. So a week with today carefully filled in and tomorrow empty still falls back to the stored start, and the card now says which day it could not answer for rather than printing four numbers that look derived. Filling in today and seeing an alarm that ignores it is how a wired-up calendar looks unwired.

It also knows when the morning does not fit. A 07:00 anchor and ninety minutes of morning wants you up at 05:05, which is true and is not a thing anyone does before a hike — so it says the chain does not fit and prices the one elastic block: drop the manuscript and it is 05:50, move those minutes to later in the day. If the phone hour has already gone it reports the slip and both ways out instead of shaving the sleep to make it close.

The day adapts to the week. The nine rows used to be the same nine rows every day, which is a fair model of a life with one fixed shift and a fiction for anyone whose Tuesday is eaten by clinic. The board now reads the Week: training is the week's call, so a day it dealt no session does not ask for one, and the evening is measured — shadowing that runs to 22:00 does not leave an hour of anatomy in the day, so it is not asked for. The card says what ate it: the blocks, the hours committed, and what is actually left after the last one. The arrival rule is never evicted — it decides how the night goes — but its minutes are counted first, and the blocks behind it compete for what is actually left. Whatever the board asks for after the last commitment always fits between then and the phone hour, and there is a test saying so.

And the day has a now. The nine rows looked identical at 06:00, at lunchtime and at 21:00 — so "after the shift" sitting on screen while you get ready for work read as a demand rather than as later. The group holding the next thing actually owed is marked; the ones behind you go quiet. It is next() that decides, so no second clock can disagree with the list it is labelling, and ticking something moves the marker by itself.

Three rows answer themselves, and say so. Anki reads the sync, training reads the grind board, anatomy reads the minutes logged on Anatomy. Each row names the board that answered it, and is locked only once that board actually has. A feed that has not run is silence, not a no — so an unreported row stays tappable and you can log it yourself, and the row then says you said so instead of pretending the sync did. These four were once locked on the promise of a reading nothing ever took: untappable and unread, which is to say impossible to complete. Spanish is a plain tap, because nothing in this hub measures it.

A tick always wins. If you trained on a day the week called rest, the week was wrong about that afternoon and the tick is the fact. After it comes a board that affirms — held, sabbath, not-asked and evicted are all statements about what a day was expected to hold, and a recorded session is a statement about what it held. Zero reps is never a recorded miss: the sync running and finding nothing is not the same as an answer, and only an answer counts.

The sabbath is declared, and held. One weekday, picked on the Season page. Training, study, Anki and the manuscript are held on it — nothing accrues, no streak breaks, the numbers still render — and prayer is deliberately not held, because it is what the day is for. Declare none and nothing is held: it is not assumed for you. It is held on the day it fell, not only while it is today: asking the clock which weekday it is answered for the present moment on every past day, so a kept sabbath showed up in the week grid a day later as one more blank.

The page notices the day turned. A checklist painted once at load is a picture of the day it was painted for, and on a phone that tab is never closed — iOS restores it from its back-forward cache without running a line of script, so day two opened on day one's render, ticks and all. Returning to the tab, refocusing the window and the boundary itself all force a repaint now. It is day.js that notices, beside the rollover it depends on, so it fires on both edges — the hub's 05:00 day and the calendar date the datelines print — rather than on midnight alone, which left the season card five hours stale.

And a change from another device repaints rather than reloads. A tick made on the Mac used to sit in the cloud until the phone's tab was reloaded by hand; when it did land it threw the whole page away, losing the scroll position and anything half-typed. A page can now tell sync.js it will redraw itself, and the Standing and the Season both do. The reload is still what happens to a page that has not said so — a page that has not claimed responsibility for repainting would otherwise sit there showing a store it no longer has.

Four states, not three. Green done, gold outlined for a floor day, red missed, and grey for a day the board was simply never told about. Grey never counts against you, and nothing turns red inside the day it is owed — red is retrospective only. Gold filled means held, which is what it already means everywhere else here.

And every row says its state in a word. The card was drawing six states with three glyphs — a tick, a dash, a middle dot — and naming none of them, so a sabbath turned four rows gold with a dot in the box and nothing on screen said why. sabbath, held, not asked, floor, done, or the name of the board that answered it. The two holds are deliberately different words: a condition you declared stands until you clear it, the sabbath comes round every week, and "held" alone does not tell you which you are looking at. The word is built in one place so the Standing and the Season cannot describe the same gold box differently. The reason the card looks the way it does — the sabbath, the committed hours, what is left after the last block — now sits above the rows it explains rather than below the ninth one, which on a phone was off the bottom of the screen.

The week is counted, never chained. Seven days of pips under each pillar, with the sentence beside them counted from those pips rather than stored next to them. There is no streak number anywhere in this design and there is a test saying there never will be: "most days this week" survives a bad Tuesday and "never break" does not.

The check-in is the only screen that reviews anything. On day 21, 42, 63 and 84 it opens with the rules you changed off-window, then the counts since the last one, then the claim the whole season is for: the median heaviness on days both prayers were kept against the days they were not. Under eight logged day-closes it says so and reads nothing — a number from four evenings is worse than no number. And it prints the hedge at the same size as the figure: which way it runs, this cannot say.

The rules shut at 21:00 and open on a check-in day. That is not a lock — it is your own store and you know where the editor is. What it buys is that a change made at 23:00 is stamped, and the next check-in opens with it.

A bout is one round, from the loss to the reset. It opens the moment you press lost that round and closes when you press reset done. What gets counted is the gap between those two presses — how fast you came back, the skill that survives a bad night — not how long the round lasted, and not days clean, which makes every failure total and is therefore the number most worth faking. A bout logged the next morning is marked as recalled and kept out of that median instead of reporting a nine-hour reset.

What counts as losing a round is yours to write, and the hub will not guess. That is a fact about you, not about the page, and a definition invented here would be the hub telling you what your own problem is. The field is on the Season page above the count; until it is filled in, the page says out loud that it is counting something it cannot name. It is kept on that device only, beside the bouts — a sentence naming the behaviour is more revealing than a list of the times it happened, and every ct_ key rides the sync to the cloud in plaintext.

Two buttons for two different moments. Lost that round opens a bout, after. I'm already derailing fires before one — and it answers with the body: get outside, or into the shower, or cold water, depending on the hour. Anything owed comes after the interrupt, is the smallest thing still open rather than the next in order, and is not offered at all once the night has been answered — at that point the honest reply is bed, and it says what time. It used to read the day's list and answer a bad night with a sixty-minute anatomy block, which is the opposite of the protocol printed six inches above it: stop, physical interrupt, one line of truth, prayer, then the next block. Both surfaces raise the same alert from the same function, so they cannot drift.

The bouts never leave the device. They live under a __local key, which means two things worth knowing rather than discovering: no backup will ever restore them, and a count read on the laptop cannot see what the phone logged — so the figure always says which device it came from. Only a tally syncs, never a record.

And a mis-tap can be taken back. The count sits on the Season page whenever there is anything logged — not only on a check-in day, because a bout recorded by accident that stays invisible for three weeks turns up inside the one review it would most distort. Beside it is remove the last one, confirmed. It takes back the most recent bout only and recomputes the tally that syncs: it is an undo for a fat thumb, not a delete tool, and rewriting the middle of the log is deliberately not offered. A log you cannot correct is not a more honest log — it is a wrong one, and a number you know to be wrong is one you stop reading.

The day's order is on the front door. While a season is live the Standing carries the nine rows in sequence, tonight's four numbers on one line, and both lap buttons — so the page you already open first is the one that answers what is next. A floor day still wins outright: there the card is not rendered at all, because adding nine rows under the one thing would undo the only thing a floor day does.

And the night has a third state. Past the phone hour it says how late and what bed now is actually worth. But close enough to the alarm it stops offering a bedtime and says the night is gone, because bed now is 12m at 05:53 is true and useless, and advice that does not hold is the same failure as a schedule that always fits.

The ex rule is read, not restated. The No list says closed unless she initiates; the date that makes it concrete lives on the Wait, computed from the day the message actually went out, and the Season reads it rather than keeping a second copy. Two ninety-day counters with different start dates would be believed selectively at 02:00. With no send date recorded it says so and sends you there, instead of counting from a day it invented.

Anything decided gets written down. One line, on the Season page, stamped with the day and whether the rules were open at the time. Not to catch you — so the check-in opens with what actually happened rather than what you remember happening.

The backlog gets a horizon. Anki's queue is read against the median of your own reviewed days, from the year of history anki_sync.py backfills, so the pile has a date instead of a feeling. Under seven reviewed days it says so rather than inventing one, and the daily cap is set in Anki — the hub reports the limit and cannot enforce it.

Nothing was deleted

Pages that were folded into a place still exist at their old address and send you to the right panel, so an old bookmark or a link in a journal entry still works. The v1 planning documents are kept and labelled in the Archive.

The source documents are kept as written

Two plans were written outside the hub and are published here unedited — the full life plan under the Plan, and the Lublin cash-flow document under Money. Neither is corrected to agree with the dashboard. Where a figure in one of them did not survive being checked, the amendment lives in the plan's Corrections list instead, with the working shown.

That split is deliberate: a source document rewritten to match the dashboard stops being a record of what was actually thought, and then there is nothing left to check the dashboard against.

Moneyone number, several currencies

There is one answer to "what is net worth"

You record it in one place — the Net worth panel, one snapshot a month, five minutes on the first Sunday. Everything else reads that: the figure at the top of Debt, the tile on the Standing, the runway on the Vault itself.

It did not always. Debt printed a fixed number under the words "net worth today" that had not moved since August, while the Net worth panel of the same page held the real thing — so two tabs of one page disagreed. Anything that shows the figure now also shows which month it came from, and says so plainly when it is more than a couple of months old.

Every amount carries its currency

Earnings are in dollars and some of what you hold stays in złoty, so a snapshot records which one it is in. Rates are typed in by hand and stored with the date you set them — there is no live feed, because a number fetched at render time would quietly rewrite your history every time you opened the page. A rate more than a month old says so rather than passing for current.

When a rate is missing, the hub refuses the sum instead of guessing. You will see "that holding is not counted" rather than a total that is silently short — a quiet 1:1 is how a fourfold error becomes a number you trust.

Two money pages, two different questions

Debt is fifteen years long and in dollars: caps, forgiveness, refinancing, what the whole road costs. School year is nine months long and in złoty: whether November's paper lot can be bought without the month going red. Both are true, and neither answers the other's question, which is why they are separate tabs rather than one page.

The pots sit under School year — Cushion is the Lublin operating floor, Nest the long household buffer, Move the interview and relocation line. Cushion and Nest are kept apart on purpose, so a bad month in Lublin cannot spend the thing that exists to absorb a bad month in New York.

Trainingthe week decides, not the weekday

Which day carries which session is the calendar's answer

The Week reads your calendar, finds the hours that are actually free and lays the block's six sessions into them. The Grind board then draws what the Week found — so a Wednesday shows Strength A when that is where the week put it, and the heading, the instruction line and the checklist all move with the session. What stays with the weekday is the shape of the day around it: the wake, the shift, the meals, the Anki block.

The board's own fixed timetable — Monday is Strength A because it is a Monday — is the fallback for a device that has never read a calendar, and it is a whole-week fallback. It is not consulted day by day.

Six sessions do not fill seven days

One day of every planned week comes back empty — the one your calendar took. That day says "no session — the week had no room" and offers no tick, on the board and on the Week alike. It does not quietly fall back to the session its weekday is named after: doing that put a second Strength C in the week while the real one sat on the day the planner had found room on, and both rows wrote the same record, so clearing one cleared the other.

The daily block, the posture resets and the house task are not the session and still stand on that day.

Peoplea wait, with the rules already decided

The Wait computes what the hour permits

The Wait holds one reach-out and the limit agreed around it: one ask, one bump, one week. It stores four dates and nothing else — when the message went out, whether the bump was used, whether she replied, and the day the other option was closed. The window, the deadline and the floor are all derived from the first of those, so the page can say hold or the window is open for the hour you are actually reading it in. The bump button is disabled outside the window; it is the gate, not a reminder of one.

The figure it prints is not days elapsed, it is her waking hours. She is nine hours ahead in summer and eight in winter, and the gap is read off both wall clocks each time rather than stored, so it survives either side's clock change. Waking up to no reply is four of her waking hours, not a day of silence, and the page says so. If the browser cannot resolve the zone it prints not counted rather than assuming an offset — the same rule the money layer follows on a missing rate.

It sends nothing. Everything on it is a record or a refusal.

The mornings are counted on a wall

While a wait is live, a third control joins the stack under the menu button on every page — a dark pill carrying the day number, with a rust dot when this morning is not on the wall yet. It opens the mornings: a prisoner's tally, one mark scratched into the wall for each morning you woke into a day with no news in it, the fifth of each five laid diagonally across the other four. Each day carries its own line and one thing to actually do, fixed to the day number rather than shuffled — a line that changes when you reload is a feed.

It keeps only three things: which mornings were marked, which of the eight steps are ticked, and the day you accepted the silence. Day one, the week, and whether she replied all come from The Wait — so using the bump moves the deadline on the wall too, and a reply recorded on either surface is a reply on both. Ticking "she wrote back" writes the same field the Wait page writes.

However it ends, the panel gives you the same eight steps in the same order, starting with canopy and finishing with the phone downstairs. What differs between them is the weather, not the list. With no send date stored the button is still there — it reads Not started and the panel asks for the one date everything else is derived from, rather than drawing a wall counting from a day it invented. A folded page's own tab row gives up the width the pill sits in, the same way the destination row gives up the menu button's — a row that does not reach under a floating control cannot have a tab under it at any scroll position, which padding could never promise.

On a phone

The installed app runs edge to edge

Added to the home screen, the hub fills the whole screen — under the clock and the battery at the top, and under the home indicator at the bottom. That is deliberate: it is what makes it look like an app rather than a web page in a frame.

It costs one thing, which is that the page has to keep clear of both edges itself. Content starts below the status bar, the bar behind it is opaque so nothing scrolls into view underneath the clock, and the tab buttons sit above the home indicator rather than behind it. If the top ever looks cut off again, it is because the phone is still holding an old copy — the hub checks its own version when you come back to it and reloads once, so closing the app and reopening it is the fix.

The menu button has a lane of its own

The round ☰ at the top right opens the drawer, and it floats above the page rather than sitting in it — so everything else has to keep out of its way. Every masthead reserves the space, and the row of section tabs stops short of it rather than scrolling underneath, which is what used to hide whichever tab happened to be beneath it.

That is why it stays at the top rather than sitting lower down where there often looks to be more room: a floating control moved down the page does not stop covering things, it just covers different ones — on most pages, the headline. It is 52px across, which is comfortably above the size a thumb needs.

If something goes wrong

Back it up, and know what a backup holds

Settings → Backup writes every store in this browser to one file, and reads one back. It reads what is actually there rather than a list someone has to remember to update.

Three things are deliberately left out: sync bookkeeping, the device-only vault key, and your calendar token. A downloaded file is less private than the cloud, not more, and a credential does not belong in one. Sign in to the calendar again after a restore.

Restoring is two steps. The file is described first — how many stores, from when, how many would be overwritten and which — and only then are the buttons offered. Merge adds only what is missing; Replace overwrites. Neither deletes a store the file does not mention.

The pictures

What is on the walls, and why those

Three groups, because the brief asked for three things. The anatomy is Joseph Maclise's Surgical Anatomy of 1859 — hand-tinted lithographs of the opened thorax, drawn from dissection at a time when nobody could yet operate inside one. The heart in Plate I is the organ this whole hub is pointed at, drawn eighty years before anyone learned to stop it and start it again. Then the Polish history painting, and then the Hudson River School for the long view — including Cole's Course of Empire, five canvases about a republic that grows, gorges and is pulled down.

Each wide page carries one in the margin, chosen to suit it: the thorax on the Plan, Grunwald on Money, Chełmoński's girl in the late-summer field on Rest. They appear on a desktop, where there is a margin to put them in, and not on a phone, where there is not.

Cole painted these five as one argument — a republic that grows, gorges, and is pulled down — and they are kept in his order rather than sorted into the rest.

All thirty are public domain — the painters and the anatomist have been dead well over a century — and they ship as files rather than sealed with the rest of the site, because a public painting is not your content. To add another, put the file in plates/ and name it in a slot; there is no CSS to edit.

Keeping this page true

This page is updated with every change

Every change to the hub — a new panel, a new page, a rule that moves, a store that starts holding something it did not — updates this Guide in the same commit. Not afterwards, and not when someone remembers. A guide that is allowed to lag is worse than none, because it is believed.

Two things already refuse to go stale on their own: the list of places above is generated from the site map rather than typed, and the backup line counts the stores actually in this browser. Everything else on this page is written by hand, and a hand-written description is the thing that goes quietly wrong. The rule is written down for the same reason the visit rule is: an intention that is not recorded is not a rule.
